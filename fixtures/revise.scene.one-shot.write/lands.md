Four o'clock, and the light went up at four in November whether or not the dark had said so yet. Ines went up with it. Ninety-one stairs; she had stopped counting them in the first year and started again in the third, not for the number but because the count was a thing to say on the way, and Wren liked a voice on the stairs.

The eighty-fourth was loose. It had been loose when she came and it would be loose when she left, and between those two facts she had learned to put her weight on the outer edge, where the iron met the wall, without any part of her deciding to. Wren took it in one long stride that skipped it altogether. He had worked that out for himself, the first winter, and she had watched him do it and said nothing, and that had been the shape of most of what passed between them since.

The lamp room took her in the order it always took her. Wick first, trimmed square with the shears that lived on the sill and nowhere else. Then the reservoir, paraffin poured to the brass line and no further. Then the glass, which was never clean and was cleaned regardless, pane by pane, her breath ahead of the rag.

Then the weight. She put her back into the crank and counted aloud to sixty, because a weight wound short runs out in the small hours. Below her the clockwork took it up and began, without hurry, to spend it.

Then the log. Date, hour, wind, glass, sea, remarks. Six things in six columns, and never a column left empty, though remarks was most nights the word nothing, written out whole, because a dash could have been anyone's.

She ate at nine and was back at one, and at the one o'clock turn the wind came round off the land and brought her what the land had been doing all day.

It came in at the seams of the lamp-room windows with a note she could have sung, and had sung, some nights, to see whether Wren would lift his head. Tonight she trimmed and wound and logged and did not sing. Fresh, she wrote. Nothing.

Toward four the glass steadied and the sea went to something she would need a word for in the morning. She did not find it then. She stood in the gold with her hands at her sides and the dog against her boots, ninety-one stairs under them both, and did not have to say it was enough to know that it was.
