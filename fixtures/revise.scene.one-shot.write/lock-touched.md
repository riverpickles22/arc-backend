Four o'clock, and the light went up at four in November whether or not the dark had said so yet. Ines went up with it. Ninety-one stairs; she had stopped counting them in the first year and started again in the third, not for the number but because the count was a thing to say on the way.

The eighty-fourth stair had a give in it that she had long since stopped noticing, and the dog skipped it outright, the way he had since his first winter here.

The lamp room took her in the order it always took her. Wick first, trimmed square with the shears that lived on the sill and nowhere else. Then the reservoir, paraffin poured to the brass line and no further. Then the glass, pane by pane, her breath ahead of the rag.

Then the weight, and sixty turns counted aloud, because a weight wound short runs out in the small hours.

Then the log. Date, hour, wind, glass, sea, remarks. Never a column left empty, though remarks was most nights the word nothing, written out whole.

She ate at nine and was back at one, and at the one o'clock turn the wind came round off the land.

Tonight she trimmed and wound and logged and did not sing. Fresh, she wrote. Nothing.

Toward four the glass steadied. She stood in the gold with the dog against her boots and did not have to say it was enough to know that it was.
