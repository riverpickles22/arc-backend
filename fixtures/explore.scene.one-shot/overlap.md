Ines went up at four because the light went up at four in November, and a keeper who waited for the dark to tell her so was already late. Ninety-one stairs. She had stopped counting them in the first year and started again in the third, not because she doubted the number but because the count was a thing to say, and Wren liked a voice on the stairs.

The eighty-fourth was loose. It had been loose when she came and it would be loose when she left, and between those two facts she had learned to put her weight on the outer edge, where the iron met the wall, without any part of her deciding to. Wren took it in one long stride that skipped it altogether. He had worked that out for himself, the first winter, and she had watched him do it and said nothing, and that had been the shape of most of what passed between them since.

In the lamp room she did the things in their order. The wick first, trimmed square with the shears that lived on the sill and nowhere else; then the reservoir, paraffin poured until the float rode where the brass line said it should; then the glass, which was never clean enough and was cleaned anyway, one pane at a time, her breath on it and the rag after. Then the match. The mantle caught with the small cough it always gave and the room went gold to the corners, and she stood a moment with her hands at her sides, because the moment was part of it.

The drive weight had to be wound. She put her back into the crank and counted the turns aloud, sixty, because a weight wound short ran out in the small hours and a light that stopped turning was a light that lied. Below the crank the clockwork took the weight and began, without hurry, to spend it.

Then the log. Date, hour, wind, glass, sea, remarks. She wrote the same six things every night in the same six columns and had never once left a column empty, though remarks was most nights the word nothing, written out in full, because a dash could have been anyone's. Wind north-west, fresh. Glass falling a little. Sea moderate. Nothing.

She went down at nine to eat and up again at one, and it was at the one o'clock turn that Wren was waiting on the landing, chin on the top stair, before she had so much as reached for her boots. She had not said anything. She had not moved in the chair. He had read the hour off her the way he read the weather off her, from something in how she sat, and gone ahead to the landing to be found there, and she found him.

Toward four the wind dropped to nothing at all, which was the alarming thing, and she went to the glass and stood there until it picked up again.
